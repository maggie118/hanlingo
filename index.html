Hello! / Excuse me.
你好！
Nǐ hǎo
0:00 / 0:00

💡 Cultural Note

你好 is the safest, most neutral "hello" in Mandarin — it works with strangers, elders, and friends. Don't use 嗨 (hāi, borrowed from English "hi") with people older than you; it can sound too casual. When entering a small shop, 你好 is more polite than staying silent.

🚕 REAL SCENARIO
Can you take me to this address?
请带我去这个地址。
Qǐng dài wǒ qù zhè ge dì zhǐ.
0:00 / 0:00

💡 Cultural Note

Most taxi drivers in China don't read English — even in Shanghai or Beijing. Always keep your destination written in Chinese (from your hotel card, a saved screenshot, or Baidu/Amap). Just show the screen plus play this sentence, and you'll get there with zero confusion. 请 at the start makes it politely formal, which taxi drivers appreciate.

🎯 FREE
How much is this?
这个多少钱？
Zhè ge duō shǎo qián?
0:00 / 0:00

💡 Cultural Note

In open-air markets and tourist streets, haggling with a smile is completely normal — but supermarket and mall prices are fixed. A friendly opener: point + 这个多少钱？, then reply with pián yi yì diǎn (a little cheaper). The particle 吗 at the end turns a statement into a yes/no question; keep your tone rising, just like in English.

🎯 FREE
Do you speak English?
你会说英语吗？
Nǐ huì shuō Yīng yǔ ma?
0:00 / 0:00

💡 Cultural Note

Younger Chinese (< 30) often learn English in school but are shy to speak — don't mistake a quiet answer for "they can't". Ask slowly with 你会说英语吗？ and if they say 一点点 (yī diǎn diǎn = a little bit), switch to simple words + gestures. Also note: in China, "English" is always Yīng yǔ — never "American" or "Australian".

🎯 FREE
I would like to order this.
我要点这个。
Wǒ yào diǎn zhè ge.
0:00 / 0:00

💡 Cultural Note

In Chinese restaurants, waiters are extremely used to customers simply pointing at a photo menu and saying 我要点这个 — no long polite sentence needed. The verb 点 is specifically "to order (food)"; it's more natural than the literal verb mǎi (to buy/ purchase). If you want two portions, add 两个 (liǎng ge) before 这个.
